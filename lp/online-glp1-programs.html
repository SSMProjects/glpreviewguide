If you've looked into GLP-1 medications, you've probably seen online prices that look almost too simple: "$39 your first month," "$139 to start." After a few weeks of reading the fine print, here's what I learned. The first-month price is a teaser. The monthly total depends on three things: the program fee, the medication, and whether insurance pays for any of it.

Here's how those pieces fit together, so you can compare programs on the real number.

Getting a prescription weight-loss medication used to mean a primary care visit, a referral, a pharmacy run and a lot of phone calls with your insurer. Online programs have folded most of that into a phone. You answer a health questionnaire, meet a licensed clinician by video or message, and if you're prescribed, the medication ships to you.

It's easier. But "easier" isn't the same as "the same everywhere." The programs I compared differ a lot in how much doctor time you get, which medications they offer, and what support you get after the first box arrives.

How online GLP-1 programs work

  1. Health questionnaire (about 5–10 minutes)Your weight history, conditions and current medications. Programs use it to screen out people for whom GLP-1s aren't safe.
  2. Clinician reviewA licensed doctor, NP or PA reviews your answers, sometimes over video, and may order lab work. They decide whether a prescription is appropriate. Many people aren't prescribed, and good programs refund you if you're not.
  3. Medication and follow-upIf you're prescribed, the medication ships from a licensed pharmacy or you pick it up locally. Check-ins adjust the dose over several months.

What the research says, and doesn't

In large clinical trials, adults using brand-name GLP-1 medications for weight management, along with diet and exercise, lost more weight on average than people on placebo. In one 68-week semaglutide trial, the average was about 15% of body weight versus about 2% on placebo.1 Individual results vary a lot, and some people lose little.

Those results come from FDA-approved medications. Compounded semaglutide and tirzepatide aren't FDA-approved, haven't been through the same review, and aren't generic versions of brand-name drugs.2 The FDA has also restricted routine compounding now that the brand-name drugs are no longer in shortage.

Before you sign up anywhere: my checklist

  • Clinicians are licensed in your state, and the program says who they are.
  • Medication comes from a licensed U.S. pharmacy, and the program tells you whether it's brand-name or compounded.
  • You can see the full monthly price, not just the first month.
  • There's a refund if you aren't prescribed.
  • You can reach a clinician about side effects.

Important safety information

GLP-1 medications (such as semaglutide and tirzepatide) are available by prescription only and aren't right for everyone. Your clinician decides whether a medication is appropriate for you.

  • Possible thyroid tumors, including cancer. In studies with rodents, these medications caused thyroid C-cell tumors. It isn't known whether they cause these tumors in people. Don't use them if you or a family member has had medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC), or if you have Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia syndrome type 2 (MEN 2).
  • Common side effects include nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, constipation and stomach pain.
  • Serious side effects can include pancreatitis, gallbladder problems, kidney problems, serious allergic reactions, low blood sugar (especially with other diabetes medicines), increased heart rate, and changes in vision in people with diabetes. Tell your clinician about any mood changes or thoughts of self-harm.
  • Don't use these medications if you're pregnant or planning to become pregnant.

This isn't a complete list. Read the Medication Guide for any medication you're prescribed, and talk to your clinician. Report side effects to the FDA at fda.gov/medwatch or 1-800-FDA-1088.
