What changed in 2026

Until recently, every FDA-approved GLP-1 medication for weight loss was an injection. Then the FDA approved the first once-daily GLP-1 pill for chronic weight management, which became available in January 2026. A second, different GLP-1 pill was approved in April 2026.

Both are brand-name medicines made by the drugmakers and approved by the FDA. They're not compounded copies. They work on the same pathway as the weekly shots: GLP-1, a hormone your gut releases when you eat that helps signal fullness and slows digestion.

Why some offers cost $69 (and what the FDA says)

Many low-price GLP-1 ads are for compounded drugs, versions mixed by compounding pharmacies. Compounded drugs are not FDA-approved, and the FDA doesn't review them for safety, effectiveness or quality before they're sold.

In 2026, the FDA sent warning letters to dozens of telehealth companies over how they marketed compounded GLP-1 drugs, for example by calling them "generic" or implying they're the same as brand-name medicines. Compounded options can be legal, and some people choose them. You just deserve to know which one you're getting.

Who may qualify

These medications are approved for adults who have obesity (a BMI of 30 or higher), or who are overweight (a BMI of 27 or higher) with at least one weight-related condition, such as high blood pressure or high cholesterol. They aren't for everyone: people with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) or with MEN 2 shouldn't use them, and they aren't for use during pregnancy. A licensed clinician makes the final call.

Important safety information

GLP-1 medications are available only with a prescription from a licensed clinician and aren't right for everyone.

  • Possible thyroid tumors, including cancer. In studies with rodents, semaglutide and similar medicines caused thyroid C-cell tumors. It isn't known whether they cause these tumors in people. Don't use them if you or a family member has had medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC), or if you have Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia syndrome type 2 (MEN 2).
  • Common side effects include nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, constipation, stomach pain, headache and tiredness.
  • Serious side effects can include pancreatitis, gallbladder problems, kidney problems, serious allergic reactions, low blood sugar (especially with insulin or sulfonylureas), increased heart rate, vision changes in people with type 2 diabetes, and depression or thoughts of self-harm. Tell your clinician right away about any of these.
  • Don't use these medications if you're pregnant or planning to become pregnant.
  • Pills must be taken exactly as directed; some must be taken on an empty stomach with a small amount of water.

This isn't a complete list. Read the Medication Guide for any medication you're prescribed. Report side effects to the FDA at fda.gov/medwatch or 1-800-FDA-1088.
