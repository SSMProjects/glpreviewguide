For decades the advice was the same: eat less, move more. For a lot of people it simply stops working in midlife — and the reason has become one of the most talked-about shifts in how modern medicine thinks about weight.

The "Eat Less, Move More" Myth

It's the advice your doctor gave you. The advice your trainer repeats. The advice every diet book is built around. And for many people over 40, it quietly stops delivering results — not because they're doing it wrong, but because something underneath has changed.

People eating carefully and walking every day, still watching the middle expand. The math doesn't add up — which means something other than calories is driving it.

The Real Reason: A Hormone Called GLP-1

Much of it comes down to a hormone your gut produces every time you eat: GLP-1 (glucagon-like peptide-1). It does three things at once — and all three tend to weaken with age.

What GLP-1 does
3-in-1

Tells your brain you're full · slows digestion so blood sugar doesn't spike · signals your body to release stored fat for energy.

In your 30s, your body responds to this appetite-and-metabolism signal at full strength. Later, that response fades. So you can eat less and still feel hungry, and the stubborn fat around the middle simply sits there. It isn't a willpower problem — it's biology. And until recently, the only medical option that targeted this pathway cost a small fortune.

Important safety information

GLP-1 medications are available only with a prescription from a licensed clinician and aren't right for everyone.

  • Possible thyroid tumors, including cancer. In studies with rodents, semaglutide and similar medicines caused thyroid C-cell tumors. It isn't known whether they cause these tumors in people. Don't use them if you or a family member has had medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC), or if you have Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia syndrome type 2 (MEN 2).
  • Common side effects include nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, constipation, stomach pain, headache and tiredness.
  • Serious side effects can include pancreatitis, gallbladder problems, kidney problems, serious allergic reactions, low blood sugar (especially with insulin or sulfonylureas), increased heart rate, vision changes in people with type 2 diabetes, and depression or thoughts of self-harm. Tell your clinician right away about any of these.
  • Don't use these medications if you're pregnant or planning to become pregnant.
  • Pills must be taken exactly as directed; some must be taken on an empty stomach with a small amount of water.

This isn't a complete list. Read the Medication Guide for any medication you're prescribed. Report side effects to the FDA at fda.gov/medwatch or 1-800-FDA-1088.
