For years there was nothing to decide. If you wanted an FDA-approved GLP-1 for weight loss, you got a weekly injection, and that was that.

Then, in January 2026, the first once-daily GLP-1 pill for weight loss reached pharmacies. A second, different pill was approved that April. Now the first question in almost every online weight-loss visit is the same one: pill or shot?

It sounds like a small choice. It isn't.

The two differ in how you take them, how much weight people lost in trials, what they cost and whether insurance might help. Here's how to think it through, and what to do if you'd rather a clinician just tell you.

Same signal, two ways to deliver it

GLP-1 is a hormone your gut releases after you eat. It tells your brain you're full and slows how quickly your stomach empties. GLP-1 medicines act on that same signal, more strongly and for longer. Many people describe the constant background thinking about food, often called "food noise," getting quieter, and smaller portions feeling like enough.

The pill and the shot work on the same pathway. The difference is delivery. A weekly shot releases the medicine slowly over seven days from a small pen you use at home. A daily pill has to survive the stomach, so you take it every day. One of the approved pills is taken first thing in the morning on an empty stomach with a sip of water, 30 minutes before food; the other can be taken any time of day, with or without food.

What the trials found

Each medicine was tested against a placebo in adults with overweight or obesity who were also given diet and exercise guidance. These are the headline averages:

13.6%average body weight lost on a daily GLP-1 pill (64 weeks)
14.9%average lost on a weekly semaglutide shot (68 weeks)

A weekly tirzepatide shot, which acts on GLP-1 and a second hormone, produced an average loss of about 21% at its highest dose over 72 weeks. On placebo, people lost about 2% to 3%.

Sources: OASIS 4 (N Engl J Med, 2025), STEP 1 (N Engl J Med, 2021) and SURMOUNT-1 (N Engl J Med, 2022). These were separate trials in different people, not a head-to-head comparison. Averages aren't guarantees: some people lose more, some less.

The best GLP-1 is the one you'll actually keep taking, month after month.

Which one fits you?

The pill may suit you if…

  • You'd rather avoid needles altogether
  • A daily routine, like a vitamin, is easy for you
  • You want the lowest starting price
  • You're paying cash

The shot may suit you if…

  • You want the largest average results seen in trials
  • Once a week is easier than every day
  • Your insurance might cover it
  • A small needle doesn't bother you

Many people are surprised by how small the needle on a modern pen is: it's short and fine, and the pen hides it. Others simply never want to think about it, and for them the pill removes the biggest reason they never started. Neither choice is the "serious" one. Side effects: mostly the same story

Because they work on the same signal, the pill and the shot share the same common side effects: nausea, diarrhea, constipation and stomach upset, usually worst in the first weeks and after each dose increase. In the pill trial above, stomach side effects were reported by 74% of people on the pill compared with 42% on placebo, mostly mild to moderate and temporary.

That's why both start at a low dose and step up slowly. The shot can also cause redness or itching where it's given. The pill asks for consistency: a missed day matters more than with a weekly shot.

Can't decide? Ask for a recommendation

That's what the clinician is for. In the online visit, you can say you want to learn about both, or simply ask which they'd recommend. They'll look at your health history, your other medications, your goals and your budget.

Who may qualify

Pill or shot, these medicines are approved for adults with a BMI of 30 or higher, or 27 or higher with a weight-related condition such as high blood pressure or high cholesterol. They aren't for everyone: people with a personal or family history of certain thyroid cancers, and people who are pregnant, shouldn't use them. A licensed clinician makes the final call.

Questions people ask

Is the pill as effective as the shot?

In separate trials, people on the weekly shots lost somewhat more weight on average than people on the daily pills. But results vary a lot from person to person, and a pill you take consistently may work better for you than a shot you put off. Your clinician can help you weigh it.

Can I switch from one to the other?

Often, yes. If one isn't working for you, or the side effects bother you, talk to your clinician about changing medication or form.

Is either covered by insurance?

At the program we reviewed, the pills are cash-pay. It offers a free insurance check for the weekly shots on commercial plans. It doesn't accept Medicare.

Does the shot hurt?

Most people describe it as a quick pinch. The pens use a short, fine needle and are designed to be used at home in a few seconds.

Do I need a prescription?

Yes. Every GLP-1 medication, pill or shot, requires a prescription. Online programs connect you with a licensed clinician who reviews your health history and decides whether a prescription is appropriate.

What if I'm not approved?

At the program we reviewed, the $39 first-month membership is refunded if a clinician finds you're not eligible for GLP-1 treatment.

Will I have to take it forever?

Not necessarily, but many people regain weight after stopping. Ask your clinician about a long-term plan before you start.

Is this the same as the $69 offers I see online?

No. Those are usually compounded drugs, which aren't FDA-approved. The prices on this page are for FDA-approved brand-name medicines, and the program we reviewed is LegitScript-certified.

The bottom line

The pill and the shot are two ways to take the same kind of medicine. The shot has shown bigger average results in trials; the pill has no needles and a lower starting price. Both are FDA-approved prescription options with real benefits, real side effects and real costs, and the first step is the same for both: a short online visit, $39 for the first month, refunded if you don't qualify.

Important safety information

GLP-1 medications are available only with a prescription from a licensed clinician and aren't right for everyone.

  • Possible thyroid tumors, including cancer. In studies with rodents, GLP-1 medicines like these caused thyroid C-cell tumors. It isn't known whether they cause these tumors in people. Don't use them if you or a family member has had medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC), or if you have Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia syndrome type 2 (MEN 2).
  • Common side effects include nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, constipation, stomach pain, headache and tiredness.
  • Serious side effects can include pancreatitis, gallbladder problems, kidney problems, serious allergic reactions, low blood sugar (especially with insulin or sulfonylureas), increased heart rate, vision changes in people with type 2 diabetes, and depression or thoughts of self-harm. Tell your clinician right away about any of these.
  • Don't use these medications if you're pregnant or planning to become pregnant.
  • Weekly injections can cause reactions where the shot is given, such as redness or itching.
  • Pills must be taken exactly as directed; some must be taken on an empty stomach with a small amount of water.

This isn't a complete list. Read the Medication Guide for any medication you're prescribed. Report side effects to the FDA at fda.gov/medwatch or 1-800-FDA-1088.
