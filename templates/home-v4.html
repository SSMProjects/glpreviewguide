What a GLP-1 does after you eat

A hormone your gut releases after a meal. It tells your brain you're full and helps regulate blood sugar.

  1. 1You eat a meal
  2. 2Your gut releases GLP-1
  3. 3Your brain gets the “full” signal

GLP-1 medications act like this hormone. Some are FDA-approved for chronic weight management in adults who meet specific criteria, others for type 2 diabetes, and they work alongside a reduced-calorie diet and more activity. Compounded versions are made by licensed pharmacies and are not FDA-approved.

Before you choose, ask

Do I need a clinician to approve treatment?

Yes. GLP-1 medications are available only through a licensed clinician. Every program on our list has one review your health history, and they may decide a GLP-1 isn't right for you.

Are compounded GLP-1s the same as brand-name ones?

No. Compounded GLP-1 medications are not FDA-approved and are not generic versions of brand-name drugs. Ask any program which medication and which pharmacy it uses.

How much do online GLP-1 programs cost?

On our list, starting prices run from about $99 a month (Trimi, 12-month plan) to $169 (MEDGm injections), with first-month offers from Pallas Health ($139) and DirectMeds ($147). Price usually depends on the medication, the dose and the plan length.

Will my insurance cover it?

Coverage for weight-management medication varies widely by plan. None of the programs on our list bill insurance directly; MEDGm and Pallas Health accept HSA/FSA.

How fast can I get started?

Most programs start with an online intake that takes a few minutes. A licensed provider reviews it, often within a day, and approved medication usually ships within a few days.

How do you make money?

Programs pay us advertising fees when readers visit them, and those fees affect where programs appear and how they are scored. DirectMeds is a paid featured placement. We never charge readers.

Important safety information

GLP-1 medications are available only through a licensed clinician and aren't right for everyone. Your clinician decides whether a medication is appropriate for you.

Possible thyroid tumors, including cancer. In studies with rodents, these medications caused thyroid C-cell tumors. It isn't known whether they cause these tumors in people. Don't use them if you or a family member has had medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC), or if you have Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia syndrome type 2 (MEN 2).

Common side effects include nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, constipation and stomach pain.

Serious side effects can include pancreatitis, gallbladder problems, kidney problems, serious allergic reactions, low blood sugar (especially with other diabetes medicines), increased heart rate, and vision changes in people with diabetes. Tell your clinician about mood changes or thoughts of self-harm.

Don't use these medications if you're pregnant or planning to become pregnant.

This isn't a complete list. Read the Medication Guide for any medication you're given. Report side effects to the FDA at fda.gov/medwatch or 1-800-FDA-1088.
